\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:litreview}

This chapter reviews the research that HepatoTwin builds on. Section~\ref{sec:lr-masld} summarises MASLD and the clinical evidence linking diet to liver outcomes. Section~\ref{sec:lr-dt} reviews digital twins in health and personalised nutrition. Section~\ref{sec:lr-ml} covers non-invasive assessment and machine-learning estimation of steatosis and fibrosis. Section~\ref{sec:lr-gastro} reviews computational gastronomy and recipe resources, and Section~\ref{sec:lr-reco} covers recipe recommendation and substitution. Section~\ref{sec:lr-gap} synthesises the gap addressed by this work.

\section{MASLD and Diet-Modifiable Liver Outcomes}
\label{sec:lr-masld}

\subsection{Nomenclature and clinical guidance}

A multisociety Delphi consensus replaced NAFLD with the term steatotic liver disease (SLD) and defined MASLD as SLD accompanied by at least one cardiometabolic criterion \cite{rinella2023}. MetALD describes MASLD with greater weekly alcohol intake, with thresholds of 140--350\,g per week for women and 210--420\,g per week for men. The EASL--EASD--EASO clinical practice guidelines \cite{easl2024} recommend a stepwise pathway in which non-invasive tests identify patients at risk of advanced fibrosis, and in which lifestyle change, including weight reduction, improved diet quality and limiting ultra-processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages, remains the cornerstone of management. These guidelines are used in this work as a source of dietary \emph{direction}, not as a source of effect sizes.

\subsection{Evidence for dietary and weight interventions}

The strongest evidence concerns weight loss. In a biopsy-paired cohort, Vilar-Gomez \emph{et al.}\ showed that weight loss through lifestyle modification improves steatosis, with benefit at $\geq 5\%$ of body weight, improvement in inflammation at 7--10\%, and NASH resolution and fibrosis regression at $\geq 10\%$ in a dose-dependent manner \cite{vilargomez2015}. For dietary patterns the evidence is more nuanced. A meta-analysis of randomised trials reported small but significant reductions in AST and GGT with the Mediterranean diet but no significant effect on ALT \cite{sangouni2022}. Comparisons of the Mediterranean diet with low-fat diets found no significant difference in liver enzymes or liver fat \cite{xiong2024}, and reviews of Mediterranean diet and calorie restriction describe favourable effects on steatosis-related outcomes \cite{haigh2022}. A meta-analysis of dietary interventions further reported that fasting interventions reduced ALT, while low-carbohydrate high-fat diets and omega-3 supplementation showed no significant change \cite{stern2026}. Structured exercise is associated with reductions in liver fat independently of weight loss \cite{stine2023}.

Nutrient-level studies provide mechanistic support. Saturated-fat overfeeding increases liver fat more than unsaturated-fat or simple-sugar overfeeding \cite{luukkonen2018}, and the effect of fructose on NAFLD markers appears largely dependent on whether energy intake is also in surplus \cite{chiu2014}. Observational meta-analyses associate coffee consumption with lower risk of advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis \cite{liu2015,ebadi2021}, but these associations are not interventional effects.

\subsection{The evidence-granularity problem}

A recurring theme is that evidence is reported at the level of weight-change thresholds, nutrients or whole dietary patterns, never at the level of an individual recipe. A single recipe is not equivalent to a dietary pattern maintained for weeks, and extrapolating a pattern-level effect to one dish is unjustified. Evidence therefore has to be graded by strength, observational findings must be separated from intervention effects, and recipes that cannot be mapped to any evidenced category must be flagged as having insufficient evidence instead of being scored by guesswork. This principle underpins the evidence layer of HepatoTwin.

\section{Digital Twins in Health and Personalised Nutrition}
\label{sec:lr-dt}

Katsoulakis \emph{et al.}\ \cite{katsoulakis2024} define a health digital twin as a computational representation of an individual that is updated with new patient data and used to simulate the effect of interventions, in contrast to a one-off prediction paired with a static visualisation. Their scoping review shows that the field is active but that many reported systems do not fully satisfy these criteria.

In nutrition, Joshi \emph{et al.}\ \cite{joshi2023} reported an open-label randomised trial in 319 people with type~2 diabetes in which digital-twin-guided nutrition, selecting foods by their predicted post-meal glucose response, improved liver-fat scores and MRI-PDFF over one year. The Horizon Europe project ARTEMIS is building virtual twins of MASLD from retrospective data on about 7{,}700 patients across European centres, including cardiovascular event prediction \cite{artemis}. A recent student abstract proposes a digital twin of diet-influenced biomarker trajectories \cite{idtdiet}. These works model patient state, biomarkers and progression, or select foods by predicted physiological response, but none ranks \emph{existing recipes} against evidence-graded, patient-specific objectives that also account for cuisine and practicality.

\section{Non-Invasive Assessment of Steatosis and Fibrosis}
\label{sec:lr-ml}

\subsection{Elastography, CAP and public cohorts}

Vibration-controlled transient elastography provides the Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) as a continuous, non-invasive measure of hepatic steatosis and liver stiffness as a measure of fibrosis. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) incorporated FibroScan examinations in its 2017--March 2020 and 2021--2023 cycles, together with laboratory biomarkers, anthropometry and 24-hour dietary recall linked by participant identifier \cite{nhanes_lux}. This makes NHANES a freely accessible cohort in which a steatosis state model can be built alongside dietary information. Its principal limitation is that it is cross-sectional, with no repeated imaging. CAP also has an upper instrument limit of 400\,dB/m, which right-censors some observations. Archived NHANES~III ultrasound images, re-read to grade hepatic steatosis and linked to mortality files, offer a route to external and outcome-linked validation in a different era \cite{nhanes3}.

\subsection{Clinical scores}

Simple indices estimate steatosis from routine measures. The Fatty Liver Index combines triglycerides, BMI, GGT and waist circumference \cite{bedogni2006}, and the Hepatic Steatosis Index uses ALT/AST ratio, BMI, sex and diabetes status \cite{lee2010}. For fibrosis, the FIB-4 index uses age, AST, ALT and platelet count \cite{sterling2006}, and the EASL--EASD--EASO pathway applies FIB-4 cut-offs to triage patients towards further testing \cite{easl2024}. These scores serve both as baselines against which machine-learning models should be compared and as sources of engineered features.

\subsection{Machine learning, explainability and calibration}

Gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost} are widely used for tabular clinical prediction because they capture non-linear interactions among anthropometric and biochemical variables. SHAP values \cite{lundberg2017} attribute a prediction to input features and satisfy an additivity property, providing both global and patient-level explanations; however, such attributions describe model behaviour and do not establish causal or clinical validity. For probabilistic outputs, post-hoc calibration by Platt scaling \cite{platt1999} or isotonic regression is common, while split conformal prediction \cite{vovk2005} provides distribution-free prediction intervals with a target coverage. Rigorous evaluation additionally requires fitting imputation and encoding on training data only, since preprocessing before the train/test split leaks information, and avoiding predictors derived from the same examination as the target.

\section{Computational Gastronomy and Recipe Resources}
\label{sec:lr-gastro}

Computational gastronomy applies data science to make food computable \cite{bagler2024}. Foundational work includes the flavour network and the food-pairing hypothesis \cite{ahn2011}, an analysis of food pairing in regional Indian cuisines \cite{jain2015}, and FlavorGraph, a food--chemical graph for representing foods and recommending pairings \cite{park2021}. Resources developed in this line of work include RecipeDB, which structures recipes with their ingredients, processes, utensils, cuisines and nutritional information; FlavorDB, which records flavour molecules associated with ingredients; and food--disease and spice--disease association resources (DietRx and SpiceRx). These resources are powerful for exploration, but they are not designed to judge whether a recipe suits the health state of a specific patient. Flavour-compound profiles in particular cannot reliably predict the flavour of a fully cooked, multi-ingredient dish, and the food--disease associations are largely literature-mined and observational, so they are best kept separate from graded intervention evidence.

Nutrient composition is typically obtained from reference databases such as USDA FoodData Central, and dietary quality can be summarised with the Healthy Eating Index-2020 \cite{shamswhite2023} using food-pattern equivalents. Two further resources are relevant to future extensions: FoodProX estimates the degree of food processing from nutrient profiles \cite{menichetti2023}, and the International Tables of Glycemic Index and Glycemic Load \cite{atkinson2021} support glycaemic-load estimation.

\section{Recipe Recommendation and Ingredient Substitution}
\label{sec:lr-reco}

Health-aware food recommendation and recipe modification have been approached through knowledge graphs of food, which identify plausible ingredient substitutions \cite{kgsubstitution2021}, surveys of substitution methods \cite{subsurvey2025}, and the use of large language models to optimise substitutions, for example to raise phytochemical content \cite{emerson2024}. Such approaches generate or modify recipes, which raises difficulties: a substituted dish may no longer taste right, be culturally appropriate or be practical, and the health effect of the modified recipe is rarely evidenced. HepatoTwin therefore takes a deliberately different position. It treats existing recipes as a candidate space and evaluates them against several criteria at once, an approach closer to multi-criteria decision analysis than to generation. Transparent weighted scoring, health gating, sensitivity analysis on the weights, diversity re-ranking and constraint-based meal planning provide interpretability that black-box recommenders typically lack.

\section{Research Gap}
\label{sec:lr-gap}

The literature reviewed above provides complementary pieces: structured recipe resources, graded evidence linking diet to liver outcomes, and digital-twin methods for modelling patient state. These have largely been studied separately. Existing digital-twin nutrition studies select foods by predicted physiological response or model disease progression, but do not treat a recipe corpus as a structured candidate space. Existing recipe resources support querying and exploration, but not patient-specific evaluation. Clinical evidence supports pattern-level and threshold-level recommendations, but does not by itself provide a framework for judging complete recipes.

We did not identify a framework that combines (i)~health relevance derived from a patient's current state and evidence-graded dietary objectives, (ii)~cuisine compatibility reflecting the patient's culinary context, and (iii)~practical cooking feasibility, within a transparent multi-criteria framework for ranking existing recipes. This thesis addresses that gap by connecting a patient-specific liver digital twin to an evidence engine and a structured recipe corpus, while stating plainly the limits imposed by cross-sectional data and by the granularity of the available clinical evidence.
